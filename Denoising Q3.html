1 · the problem

A noisy image is the clean image plus a random number at every pixel

The model is y = x + n, with every n[i,j] drawn independently from a Gaussian with mean 0 and standard deviation σ. Denoising means estimating x from y. Quality is scored against the clean target with the mean squared error W1 §4.4, usually reported as PSNR, the same number on a log scale. These two controls drive every section on the page.

Why averaging removes noise. Average k independent noise values and the result still has mean 0, but its variance drops to σ²/k. More generally a weighted sum Σ w·n has variance σ² · Σ w². A filter with weights that sum to 1 leaves a flat area's brightness alone and multiplies the noise variance by Σw², which is below 1. The catch: the same average also mixes neighbouring pixels of the picture, which blurs edges. Every denoiser is a way of managing that trade.

PSNR = 10·log10(MAX² / MSE), with MAX the largest possible pixel value (1 for floats in [0,1], 255 for uint8). Halving the MSE adds 3.01 dB. Your notebook calls peak_signal_noise_ratio(target, output): both arrays must be on the same scale and the same shape, or the number is meaningless.

2 · question 3a · the Gaussian filter

One bell-shaped set of weights, used at every pixel

A kernel of side l gives each neighbour at offset (m, n) from the centre the weight exp(−(m² + n²) / (2·sig²)), then divides by the sum of all weights so they add to 1. That division is the Wnorm in your notes' filtering equation W4 Eq. 2, and filtering is that equation: out[i,j] = Σm Σn w[m,n] · y[i+m, j+n]. For l = 5 the offsets run over −2 … 2. A colour image is filtered one channel at a time.

The kernel is separable: it is the outer product of a 1-D Gaussian with itself, because exp(−(m²+n²)/2sig²) = exp(−m²/2sig²)·exp(−n²/2sig²). The top row of numbers divided by their own sum, times the same thing as a column, gives the whole grid.

Things that go wrong in 3a (no code, just the traps): the image arrives as uint8 in 0–255, so compute in floats and convert back, or sums overflow; build the offsets so the centre of an odd-sided kernel is exactly 0; normalise, or the picture gets brighter or darker; decide what happens at the border (zero, reflect or replicate padding W5 §5.1.3), since zero padding darkens the edges; filter R, G and B separately.

3 · question 3b · the CNN denoiser

Let gradient descent choose the filters

A CNN layer is the same sliding weighted sum, with three changes: the weights are learned, there are many filters per layer, and a ReLU follows W4 Eq. 5 W4 Eq. 7. Stacking layers nests the functions W4 Eq. 6. The network fθ is trained so that fθ(noisy) is close to the clean target, on many pairs.

This is my demo network, not a template for yours. It predicts the noise and subtracts it, and its last layer has no ReLU so that the predicted noise can be negative. Your brief says ReLU after each layer and asks for “sufficient depth and complexity”; follow the brief.

loss L(θ) = (1/N) Σpixels ( fθ(y) − x )² mean squared error over a batch W1 §4.4
gradient ∂L/∂x̂ = 2 ( x̂ − x ) / N, then the chain rule back through every layer backpropagation W2 §5.2
batch average the gradient over B random pairs, not the whole set mini-batch SGD W2 §3.2
update θ ← θ − η · ∂L/∂θ (Adam rescales each coordinate by its running moments) W4 Eq. 4, W2 §4.4
Why squared error is the natural loss here. Your notes: with Gaussian residuals, “least squares maximizes the conditional likelihood” W1 §3.1. Denoising has exactly that noise model, and PSNR is a function of MSE, so minimising MSE is directly maximising the metric in your comparison cell.
